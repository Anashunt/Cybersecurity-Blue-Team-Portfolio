\# 🎣 The Greenholt Phish — Phishing Email Investigation\## 🎯 Lab ObjectiveThis investigation simulates a SOC analyst responding to a reported suspicious email that appears to originate from a known business contact.The objective is to determine whether the message is legitimate or part of a phishing campaign by analyzing the email itself, examining its headers and source information, validating the sending infrastructure, reviewing email authentication records, and safely investigating the attached file.The investigation demonstrates how multiple independent artifacts can be correlated to assess the authenticity of an email rather than relying solely on visible sender information or the appearance of the message.\---\## 📌 Scenario OverviewA sales employee reported receiving an unexpected email that appeared to originate from a known customer.Several characteristics raised suspicion, including a generic greeting, an unexpected request involving a financial transfer, and an unsolicited attachment.The employee also indicated that the communication did not match the customer's normal behavior.The message was therefore escalated to the Security Operations Center for investigation.The investigation focused on determining whether the email originated from legitimate infrastructure or whether the visible sender information had been manipulated as part of a phishing attempt.The analysis covered:\* Email subject and visible sender information\* Sender and reply addresses\* Message headers\* Originating IP identification\* IP ownership and infrastructure attribution\* SPF authentication records\* DMARC policy records\* Attachment identification\* SHA-256 file hashing\* VirusTotal investigation\* Actual attachment file-type identification\---\## 🛠️ Tools \& Techniques Used\* Email header and source inspection\* Linux command-line utilities\* DNS record lookups\* SPF analysis\* DMARC analysis\* IP ownership / infrastructure investigation\* `sha256sum`\* VirusTotal\* File-type identification\* Static attachment analysis\---\# 🔍 Analysis Walkthrough\## 1. Initial Email ReviewThe investigation began with a visual inspection of the reported email.The message content was reviewed for characteristics commonly associated with phishing attempts, including:\* Generic or unusual greetings\* Unexpected financial requests\* Requests that create urgency or pressure\* Unsolicited attachments\* Communication that differs from the sender's established behaviorThese indicators were treated as initial warning signs rather than sufficient proof of malicious activity.The investigation therefore proceeded to the message metadata and headers to determine the actual origin and authenticity of the email.\---\## 2. Subject and Sender IdentificationThe email subject was examined to extract the transaction-related reference information presented to the recipient.The visible sender information was then recorded, including the sender's display name.This distinction is important during phishing analysis because the \*\*display name shown to the user does not necessarily represent the actual mailbox responsible for sending the message\*\*.The investigation therefore continued into the raw email headers.\---\## 3. Header AnalysisThe message source and full email headers were inspected to identify the addresses used during message delivery.Particular attention was given to fields such as:```textFrom:Return-Path:Reply-To:Received:```These fields provide different pieces of information about the message.The visible `From` address was compared with the underlying delivery information to identify inconsistencies that could indicate sender impersonation.The `Reply-To` field was also examined separately because an attacker can use it to redirect the recipient's response to an address different from the apparent sender.\---\## 4. Reply-To InvestigationThe `Reply-To` header was specifically reviewed to determine where a response from the recipient would actually be delivered.This is an important phishing-analysis technique because an email can visually appear to originate from a trusted organization while directing replies to infrastructure controlled by another party.The relationship between:```textDisplay Name\&#x20;     ↓From Address\&#x20;     ↓Reply-To Address\&#x20;     ↓Return-Path```was therefore examined rather than treating the displayed sender identity as authoritative.\---\## 5. Originating IP IdentificationThe `Received` headers were then analyzed to reconstruct the email's delivery path.Because email systems can contain multiple `Received` entries, the headers were reviewed from the appropriate end of the delivery chain to identify the earliest relevant external sending host.The originating IP address extracted from the headers was then treated as an infrastructure IOC.This provided an independent artifact that could be investigated separately from the sender's claimed identity.\---\## 6. IP Ownership InvestigationThe originating IP was investigated to determine which organization or network controlled the infrastructure responsible for sending the message.The ownership information was compared with the identity claimed by the email.This step is useful when investigating impersonation because the sender may claim to represent a legitimate organization while the actual sending infrastructure belongs to an unrelated provider or network.The investigation therefore established an infrastructure-level relationship between the email and its apparent sender.\---\# 🔐 Email Authentication Analysis\## 7. SPF Record InvestigationThe domain associated with the email's `Return-Path` was identified from the message headers.An SPF lookup was then performed against this domain to retrieve its published Sender Policy Framework record.The complete SPF record was reviewed to understand which systems or services were authorized to send email on behalf of the domain.SPF analysis provided additional context for determining whether the sending infrastructure was authorized by the domain's published policy.The investigation did not rely on SPF alone; it was correlated with the originating IP and other header information.\---\## 8. DMARC Record InvestigationA DMARC lookup was then performed against the same `Return-Path` domain.The published DMARC record was examined to determine the domain's authentication and enforcement policy.The investigation considered the relationship between:```textSPF\&#x20;+DMARC\&#x20;+Sending Infrastructure\&#x20;+Email Headers```This provided a broader view of the domain's email-authentication configuration.Authentication records were treated as supporting evidence rather than as a standalone verdict because phishing investigations require correlation between multiple independent artifacts.\---\# 📎 Attachment Investigation\## 9. Attachment IdentificationThe email source was inspected to identify the attachment associated with the suspicious message.The attachment filename was extracted from the message data without opening or executing the file.This established the attachment as a separate artifact for subsequent analysis.The file was treated as untrusted throughout the investigation.\---\## 10. Safe Attachment ExtractionThe attachment was downloaded into the isolated analysis environment for static examination.The file was not executed.Instead, the investigation focused on generating a cryptographic hash and identifying its actual file format.This approach reduces unnecessary exposure to potentially malicious code while still allowing useful forensic information to be extracted.\---\## 11. SHA-256 Hash GenerationA SHA-256 hash was generated using:```bashsha256sum <attachment>```The resulting hash was recorded as a file-level IOC.The hash can be used to:\* Uniquely identify the analyzed sample\* Search threat-intelligence databases\* Correlate the file with known malware samples\* Support incident-response investigations\* Create file-based detection rulesThe hash was then used as the primary pivot for external threat-intelligence research.\---\## 12. VirusTotal InvestigationThe calculated SHA-256 hash was submitted to VirusTotal for additional intelligence.The objective was to determine whether the attachment had previously been analyzed and whether existing metadata could provide additional information about the file.The investigation reviewed available file metadata rather than executing the attachment.The VirusTotal results provided additional context about the sample, including its recorded file size.This demonstrated a common SOC workflow:```textSuspicious Email\&#x20;     ↓Attachment\&#x20;     ↓SHA-256\&#x20;     ↓Threat Intelligence\&#x20;     ↓File Metadata```\---\## 13. Actual File-Type VerificationThe final stage focused on determining the attachment's \*\*actual file type\*\*.The filename extension was not treated as authoritative because attackers can disguise malicious files by assigning them misleading extensions.The file's underlying structure and identifying characteristics were examined to determine what type of file it actually represented.This distinction is particularly important in phishing investigations because a file presented to the user as one format may internally be another format entirely.The investigation therefore compared the apparent filename with the actual binary/file structure.\---\# 🧩 Investigation CorrelationThe investigation connected the email and attachment artifacts into a single evidence chain:```textSuspicious Email\&#x20;      │\&#x20;      ├── Subject\&#x20;      │\&#x20;      ├── Display Name\&#x20;      │\&#x20;      ├── From Address\&#x20;      │\&#x20;      ├── Reply-To\&#x20;      │\&#x20;      ├── Return-Path\&#x20;      │\&#x20;      └── Received Headers\&#x20;               ↓\&#x20;       Originating IP\&#x20;               ↓\&#x20;       IP Ownership\&#x20;               ↓\&#x20;       SPF / DMARC\&#x20;               ↓\&#x20;         Attachment\&#x20;               ↓\&#x20;          SHA-256\&#x20;               ↓\&#x20;       Threat Intelligence\&#x20;               ↓\&#x20;       Actual File Type```Each artifact was investigated independently and then correlated with the others.This approach avoids reaching a phishing verdict based on a single suspicious characteristic.\---\# 🔎 Key Phishing Analysis Principles\### Display Name Is Not IdentityThe sender's display name can be manipulated easily and should not be treated as proof of the sender's identity.\### Headers Provide Delivery EvidenceThe raw message headers can reveal information that is not visible in the email client, including delivery infrastructure and routing information.\### Reply-To Requires Independent ReviewA mismatched `Reply-To` address can redirect communication away from the apparent sender and is therefore an important phishing-analysis artifact.\### Authentication Records Provide ContextSPF and DMARC records can help determine whether sending infrastructure is authorized and how the domain handles authentication failures.\### File Extensions Are Not TrustworthyThe apparent extension of an attachment does not necessarily represent its actual format.\### Hash Before Further InvestigationGenerating a cryptographic hash provides a stable identifier that can be safely used to pivot into threat-intelligence platforms without repeatedly handling or executing the sample.\---\# ✅ Summary of Findings\* The suspicious email was analyzed from both the visible message and its underlying source.\* Sender metadata was separated into display name, sender address, reply address, and return-path information.\* The message headers were analyzed to identify the originating sending infrastructure.\* The originating IP was investigated to determine its registered ownership.\* SPF and DMARC records for the relevant return-path domain were examined to assess the domain's email-authentication configuration.\* The unsolicited attachment was extracted without executing it.\* A SHA-256 hash was generated and used as a threat-intelligence pivot.\* VirusTotal was used to investigate the attachment and obtain additional file metadata.\* The attachment's actual file type was determined independently of its filename extension.\* Multiple independent artifacts were correlated to assess the legitimacy of the reported email.\---\# 📚 Skills Demonstrated\* Phishing email triage\* Email source and header analysis\* `From`, `Reply-To`, and
